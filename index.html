A stable process window across operating modesProcess TechnologyA newer line needed a laser recipe that met cut-quality requirements at both normal and reduced speeds, balancing complete cuts against dross formation.Supported a DOE with baseline sampling, a controlled power sweep, and a frequency study. Checked focus alignment when recipe adjustments produced no meaningful response.The selected condition met requirements across all required operating modes. Qualifying a change with downstream evidenceProcess TechnologyThe downstream Assembly team reported an unacceptable rate of tab-related defects.Supported dual-side tab pressing trials as a controlled 4M change across multiple lots, coordinating with Equipment, the vendor, and Quality.Quality and Assembly reviewed the samples and data. Downstream defects improved, and broader implementation was planned.
Following the equipment evidenceProcess TechnologyIncomplete cuts appeared in a cathode line's acceleration zone and persisted despite process adjustments.Used a physical check to assess suction, then reviewed the laser log after power, frequency, and focus adjustments had no effect.The missing power response redirected the investigation to the focusing hardware. A vendor specialist repaired it the next day, reinforcing the value of checking equipment data earlier.
Balancing humidity control and tab stabilityProcess TechnologyOn the anode line, more airflow for humidity control made the tabs flutter.Designed and installed a duct cover that redirected air away from the tabs while preserving airflow through a lower opening.Tab flutter decreased noticeably after installation. The improvement was observed but not quantified.

From design to repeatable manufacturing

A design becomes a product only when the manufacturing process can reproduce it consistently and at yield. My internships made me interested in the engineering between an intended result and the process conditions that repeatedly produce it.

From design to process

Studying digital hardware taught me how a chip is designed. Manufacturing taught me a different problem: a process has to reproduce that design consistently across tools, lots, and operating conditions. That is the side of engineering I found myself most drawn to.

Why process integration at TSMC

What interests me about process integration is that the answer rarely lives in one place. Process conditions, equipment behavior, inspection data, electrical results, and downstream yield have to be read together. My manufacturing experience taught me to work that way, and I want to develop that skill at semiconductor scale.

What I bring

I look across the process instead of stopping at the step where a problem becomes visible. I start from evidence, test a defined hypothesis or controlled change, and verify whether the result holds downstream before treating the work as complete. My digital hardware background also gives me context for the downstream side of process integration: the circuit and system behavior that device and interconnect variation eventually affect.
