Vision Engineering

Intern · LG Energy Solution Michigan · May 2025 – January 2026

During this internship I supported the vision inspection systems on the electrode lines. I started by learning the inspection system, then built a Raspberry Pi image-logging project at the unloader. Over time I reviewed vision and production data every day, tuned inspection recipes against false positives and false negatives, restored systems when they went down, debugged camera, trigger and timing problems at the signal level, and wrote a Python reporting tool for production updates. These responsibilities increasingly overlapped as I became more familiar with the system.

Role at a glance, in order

  • Learned the inspection system and its data.
  • Built a Raspberry Pi image-logging project at the unloader: master and slave Pis, ROI image capture, central review.
  • Reviewed vision and production data every day: defect rates by view, false positives and negatives, missed or false triggers.
  • Tuned inspection recipes against production data: about 10% fewer false positives.
  • Restored down systems with a fixed routine, found that a trigger-board reset could leave cells uninspected, and raised it with the vendor.
  • Debugged failures at the signal level with logs, an oscilloscope and a logic analyzer.
  • Wrote a Python reporting tool for hourly production updates: about 7.2% higher inspection throughput. An image-delivery extension was still in development when I returned to school.

00

Learning the inspection system

I began by learning how the inspection system worked: how the vision program inspects each view, how results reach the line, and what the defect data in SPC and Spotfire meant. I studied and supported the system. I did not design the production vision system.

What I had to learn first

Each inspection view, such as the foil area or CIS, could be wrong in two ways: a false positive, where good material is flagged, or a false negative, where a real defect passes.

Why it came first

Everything later in the internship depended on knowing what normal looked like in the data, the images and the system behavior.

01

Raspberry Pi image logging at the unloader

My first engineering project of the internship. An image-capture and logging system built from several Raspberry Pis, so the images behind an inspection result could be stored, found and reviewed.

Master and slave

Slave Pis captured images and a master Pi coordinated them over the local network, with secured remote access.

ROI image capture and logging

Capture was synchronized with the inspection system and focused on a region of interest, so the stored images were the ones that mattered for defect analysis.

Central review

Images were logged centrally on an industrial PC for review and traceability.

Why it mattered

With images tied to production metrics, a defect rate could be traced back to the images behind it, which made root-cause analysis possible later.

02

Daily vision data and system-health review

After learning the system and building the image-logging project, I became involved in reviewing vision and production data every day. Mornings were for finding out what had changed since yesterday. Afternoons were for acting on it and checking that the fix held.

Morning

  • Read the overnight defect rates by view in SPC and Spotfire and looked for shifts, not just totals.
  • Checked system health: missed or false triggers, gaps in image logging and any down events.
  • Separated false positives from false negatives, and checked the flagged cases against the stored images.
  • Decided what needed a recipe change and what needed a bench check.

Afternoon

  • Pulled images and equipment logs for the views that looked wrong, and correlated them with production metrics for root-cause analysis.
  • Adjusted the recipe, the camera, the trigger timing or the hardware, depending on what the evidence pointed to.
  • Rechecked the same data after the change, under normal production, before calling it fixed.
  • Wrote down what changed, why, and what to keep watching.

Later in the internship an automated reporting tool (section 06) sent production updates through the day.

03

Tuning the inspection program with production data

The vision program flagged defects on the anode and cathode electrodes. Whether it flagged the right ones was a data question. I read the inspection results in SPC and Spotfire for false positives (over-detection) and false negatives (missed detection), then adjusted the recipe for the view that was off.

Views behave differently

The foil area, CIS and other views each had their own failure patterns, so I read each view's results separately instead of one overall defect number.

Two errors, one trade-off

Changing an inspection threshold can trade false positives against false negatives. I checked both errors against production images before accepting a recipe change.

What it moved

Approximately 10% fewer false positives after tuning, confirmed against production images before a change was kept.

04

Recovering a vision system when it goes down

When a vision system went down, the first goal was to restore inspection safely and quickly. Recovery, however, was not the same as understanding why the failure occurred.

The routine

Turn the vision program off and on, reset the camera, reset the trigger board, then load the recipe again. After that I checked that the inspection PC and the interface PC were talking, that the PLC connection was up, and that the camera was grabbing images.

The catch I found

A trigger-board reset could lose the inspection association for cells still in flight, leaving that interval uninspected. The system looked normal again, but a stretch of cells had never been inspected.

What we changed

I raised the issue with the vendor, and the affected cells were marked NG instead of passing without inspection.

Why this story is here: a reset made the system look healthy while a stretch of cells had not been inspected. It taught me to ask what a fix costs, not only whether the system came back, and to take a problem like that to the vendor with the evidence instead of working around it.

05

Signal-level and hardware debugging

When a failure kept returning or could not be explained at the software level, I moved deeper into the system. I used equipment logs, an oscilloscope, and a logic analyzer to check trigger timing, camera behavior, communication, and cabling.

Software symptom

Missed or repeated inspections, gaps in image logging, or a failure that came back after a reset. I worked across the software, communication and hardware layers when isolating it.

Signal evidence

I used waveforms to check electrical behavior and trigger timing, and digital captures to look at communication on the inspection hardware, together with equipment logs and production data.

Interface or hardware cause

I investigated failures tied to CIS hardware and cable connections, and found and corrected a cable-related issue that was affecting system behavior.

06

Production and POR reporting automation

Once I understood the system's data and failure modes, I wanted production and POR numbers to reach people every hour without anyone pulling them by hand. I built a Python tool for it, around the constraints of the real production line and not just software tests.

What it tracked

A Python framework on Windows, built on Win32 APIs, followed production and POR data in real time from the plant's production systems.

Every hour

It reported automatically each hour, so the numbers were in front of the team during the shift, not at the end of it.

What it moved

Inspection throughput rose by about 7.2% after it went in. The absolute volume is not published.

The sections below show how the reporting workflow behaves. Room names, targets and addresses are left out, and the code itself stays private.

Problem

Production and POR numbers had to reach the right people on time, without someone pulling them by hand and without sending anything twice.

Data

Timestamped summary emails and the previous saved snapshot.

Action

Wrote a Python Windows application: a periodic cycle that skips anything already seen, parses and compares, and sends checkpointed messages through one queue.

Result

Each report goes out once, a crash resumes at the missing part, and a failed parse sends nothing. No usage numbers are published.

Never send the same report twice

Each email is identified by its timestamp. Multi-part reports are checkpointed section by section, so a crash halfway through resumes at the missing part.

Fail quietly, not wrongly

If an email cannot be parsed, nothing is sent. The failure is logged and the loop moves on to the next task instead of posting a half-right message.

Say what changed

Every report is compared with the last saved snapshot, so the message tells people what moved since the previous one.

Design details: one sender at a time, and keeping it running
07

Extending the reporting workflow to images

After the text reports were working, I started extending the same workflow to deliver line photos. This was still in development when I returned to school, so I describe it as a design, not a finished or deployed system.

  • Implemented Text reporting: the report cycle above.
  • Prototype Photo upload, batching and archive logic.
  • In progress Delivering image batches end to end.

Upload and send are separate by design

Devices upload files whenever they are ready and trigger a send once. The server replies immediately and a background worker does the rest, so a slow send never blocks the device.

Late files are not lost

A folder is queued once. If a trigger arrives while it is being processed, it is marked pending and run again afterwards. Sent files are archived into dated success or fail folders with a history log.
