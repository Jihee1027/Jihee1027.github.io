Automation · Vision systems

Vision Engineering

Intern · LG Energy Solution Michigan · May 2025 – January 2026

I started the internship building a Raspberry Pi image-logging system at the unloader. After that I looked after the vision program that inspects the anode and cathode electrodes: checking its data every day, tuning it when it flagged the wrong things, and getting systems back up when they went down. Each inspection view, such as the foil area or CIS, could be wrong in two ways, a false positive or a false negative.

Role at a glance

  • Built a Raspberry Pi image-logging system at the unloader: ROI-triggered capture, master and slave Pis, central review.
  • Reviewed vision data every morning and afternoon: defect rates by view, false positives and negatives, missed or false triggers.
  • Tuned the vision program against production data: about 10% fewer false positives.
  • Restored down systems with a fixed routine (program, camera, trigger board, recipe, then the links), found that a trigger reset left cells uninspected, and had the vendor mark them NG.
  • Wrote a Python tool that tracks production and POR in real time and reports every hour: about 7.2% higher production throughput. Also a chat relay service for reports and photos.

01

Raspberry Pi image logging at the unloader

This was my first project of the internship: an image-capture and logging system built from several Raspberry Pis, so the images behind an inspection result could be stored, found and reviewed.

Master and slave

Slave Pis captured images and a master Pi coordinated them over LAN sockets, with Tailscale for remote access.

ROI-triggered capture

Capture was triggered on a region of interest, so the stored images were the ones that mattered for defect analysis.

Central review

Images were logged centrally on an industrial PC for review and traceability.

Why it mattered

With images tied to production metrics, a defect rate could be traced back to the images behind it, which is what made root cause analysis possible later.

02

A day of vision data checks

Mornings were for finding out what had changed since yesterday. Afternoons were for acting on it and checking that the fix held.

Morning

  • Read the overnight defect rates by view in SPC and Spotfire and looked for shifts, not just totals.
  • Checked system health: missed or false triggers, gaps in image logging and any down events.
  • Separated false positives from false negatives, and checked the flagged cases against the stored images.
  • Decided what needed a recipe change and what needed a bench check.

Afternoon

  • Pulled images and equipment logs for the views that looked wrong, and correlated them with production metrics for root cause.
  • Adjusted the recipe, the camera, the trigger timing or the hardware, depending on what the evidence pointed to.
  • Rechecked the same data after the change, under normal production, before calling it fixed.
  • Wrote down what changed, why, and what to keep watching.

Reporting ran all day. The Python tool in section 05 posted production and POR every hour.

03

Tuning the vision program with data

The vision program flagged defects on the anode and cathode electrodes. Whether it flagged the right ones was a data question. I read the inspection results in SPC and Spotfire for false positives (over-detection) and false negatives (missed detection), then adjusted the recipe for the view that was off.

Views behave differently

The foil area, CIS and other views each had their own failure patterns, so I read each view's results separately instead of one overall defect number.

Two errors, one trade-off

Tightening a threshold reduces false positives and risks false negatives, and loosening does the reverse. I looked at both together, with production images, before changing a recipe.

What it moved

Approximately 10% fewer false positives, and inspection that held up better as production conditions changed.

04

When a vision system goes down

Systems went down more often than anyone liked, and a down system means parts pass through without being inspected. So I wanted a routine I could run quickly, and I wanted to know what each step really did.

The routine

Turn the vision program off and on, reset the camera, reset the trigger board, then load the recipe again. After that I checked that the inspection PC and the interface PC were talking, that the PLC connection was up, and that the camera was grabbing images.

The catch I found

The trigger board keeps a running record of cell numbers and matches each cell as it is released. When I reset the board, any cells that had not come back were lost, which meant that stretch had not been inspected. The system looked normal again, but the inspection was missing.

What we changed

I worked it out with the vendor's developer, and the program was changed so that after a trigger reset the cells whose inspection was lost are marked NG instead of passing quietly.

Why this story is here: a reset made the system look healthy while a stretch of cells had never been inspected. It taught me to ask what a fix costs, not only whether the system came back, and to take a problem like that to the vendor with the evidence instead of working around it.

If a failure kept coming back, I took it to the bench with an oscilloscope and a logic analyzer on the trigger and timing signals. That is how timing and signal problems between the trigger boards, cameras and vision software were found and fixed.

05

Python report tool

I automated the tracking so that production and POR numbers reached people every hour without anyone pulling them by hand. I built it around the constraints of the real production line, not just software tests.

What it tracked

A Python framework on Windows, built on Win32 APIs, followed production and POR in real time from FMCS data.

Every hour

It reported automatically each hour, so the numbers were in front of the team during the shift, not at the end of it.

What it moved

Production throughput rose by about 7.2% after it went in. The absolute volume is not published.

06

Signal-level and hardware debugging

Not every inspection problem was a software problem. Signal-level debugging was often what isolated the issue.

Reliability

I worked across software, communication and hardware layers when isolating failures.

Cameras and trigger boards

I troubleshot camera, trigger-board, timing-signal and communication failures using equipment logs and production data as well as bench instruments.

Oscilloscope and logic analyzer

I used waveforms to check electrical behavior and timing, and digital captures to look at communication on the inspection hardware.

CIS interface and cabling

I investigated failures tied to CIS hardware and cable connections, and found and corrected a cable-related issue that was affecting system behavior.

07

Report and photo relay service

A small Windows service that relayed automated production summaries and line photos to the right chat rooms. This section shows how it behaves. Room names, targets and addresses are left out, and the code itself stays private.

Problem

Automated production summaries and line photos had to reach the right chat rooms on time, without someone forwarding them by hand and without sending anything twice.

Data

Timestamped summary emails, the previous saved snapshot, and photo folders uploaded by devices.

Action

Wrote a Python Windows service: a 60-second cycle, skip anything already seen, parse and compare, checkpointed sends through one queue, and a supervisor and watchdog for restarts.

Result

Each report goes out once, a crash resumes at the missing part, a failed parse sends nothing, and the service restarts itself. No usage numbers are published.

Never send the same report twice

Each email is identified by its timestamp. Multi-part reports are checkpointed section by section, so a crash halfway through resumes at the missing part.

Fail quietly, not wrongly

If an email cannot be parsed, nothing is sent. The failure is logged and the loop moves on to the next task instead of posting a half-right message.

Say what changed

Every report is compared with the last saved snapshot, so the message tells people what moved since the previous one.

Upload and send are separate on purpose

Devices upload files whenever they are ready and trigger a send once. The server replies immediately and a background worker does the rest, so a slow send never blocks the device.

Late files are not lost

A folder is queued once. If a trigger arrives while it is being processed, it is marked pending and run again afterwards. Sent files are archived into dated success or fail folders with a history log.
