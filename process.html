00

Process investigation approach

The investigation followed variation across process steps, from its source to the downstream effect. The examples below show how production data, equipment behavior, and inspection evidence guided controlled changes and verification. These methods provide a foundation for semiconductor process integration work.

01

Daily production review

Daily review began with issues from the previous shift. Production trends, inspection results, alarm history, and downstream feedback were compared to identify likely causes and prioritize the next test.

Production review

  • Reviewed the previous day's issues, suspected causes, and open Process of Record items.
  • Checked Spotfire and SPC data for recurring breakage, miscuts, downtime, and abnormal shifts.
  • Prioritized issues requiring deeper investigation or same-day follow-up.

Tracing the source

  • Reviewed defect images and in-process vision footage to reconstruct how events developed.
  • Compared alarm timing and line history across handling, laser processing, inspection, and downstream steps.
  • Checked conditions before and after each event to distinguish the source from the detection point.

Action and verification

  • Shared daily defect summaries with the relevant engineering and production teams.
  • Agreed on the next test with engineers or vendors.
  • Ran controlled trials, inspected samples, and compared results with subsequent production data.

Corrective actions remained open until production data showed a sustained response.

02

Finding where variation begins

The step that detects a defect may be different from the step that caused it. Investigation combined trends, equipment history, alarms, inspection evidence, and surrounding conditions to trace the sequence of events.

Action: narrowing the source

  • Compared miscut, breakage, and downtime trends to distinguish recurring patterns from isolated events.
  • Used alarm timing, in-process inspection, and line history to locate the failure in the process flow.
  • Checked upstream and downstream conditions to look beyond the point where the line stopped.
  • Compared abnormal events with recent process changes, equipment conditions, and material behavior.
  • Monitored production and quality data after corrective actions to verify that improvements held.

Problem pattern: mechanical variation

A shift in the material path can destabilize the web, cause breakage or cracking, and disturb the material further along the line. That raises the risk of downstream defects such as miscuts.

Line history, alarms, equipment data, and vision footage helped locate each event in the process flow. Comparing mechanical disturbances with subsequent miscuts helped assess whether the events were linked.

Exact downtime figures and line-specific production data are omitted from this public version.

03

Building a stable process window with DOE

Newer lines needed a more stable operating recipe. DOE support included controlled laser adjustments, airflow trials where relevant, and microscopy of cut samples. The selected condition was checked against requirements across the line's operating modes.

Establishing the baseline

  • Coordinated planned line access and controlled sampling with Production.
  • Scheduled trials around a suitable lot transition.
  • Cleaned relevant tooling and collected baseline samples before changing the recipe.
  • Sampled multiple electrode regions to compare cut quality across the part.

Evaluating the parameters

  • Established a known production condition as the baseline for each change.
  • Ran a controlled power sweep and checked response direction and repeatability.
  • Followed the power sweep with a frequency study to assess the combined effect on cut quality.
  • Selected the most stable overall condition across quality measures.

Checking beyond the recipe

  • Checked focus alignment when power and frequency adjustments produced no meaningful response.
  • Revalidated the recipe after focus changes.
  • Repeated checks at normal production speed and under reduced-speed material-change conditions.
  • Completed sample review and qualification checks before sharing results with engineers and team leads.

Operating speeds, parameter steps, focus offsets, recipes, line identifiers, and internal acceptance limits are omitted.

04

Balancing cut quality against what comes next

A complete cut could still leave dross; reducing dross could leave the cut incomplete. Since both affected downstream quality, the selected condition had to meet all acceptance criteria and remain stable in production.

Complete cut

Enough delivered energy for a complete, stable cut without creating a new quality problem.

Insulation quality

Checked insulation protrusion and spatter against acceptance criteria using microscopy.

Dross-free target

Evaluated dross on the cut edge, with a clean edge as the target.

  • Power was the main control for a reliable complete cut.
  • Frequency was evaluated across multiple quality responses.
  • Focus was a secondary variable, used when recipe changes alone did not resolve the issue.
  • Validation meant the final condition stayed acceptable across the relevant operating modes.
05

Qualifying a process change with downstream evidence

Assembly reported an unacceptable rate of tab-related defects. The trial evaluated dual-side tab pressing on the anode line as a controlled change. Equipment and the vendor prepared the setup, Process Technology ran 4M qualification across multiple lots, and Quality and Assembly reviewed the evidence.

From problem to trial

  • Reviewed Assembly's feedback on tab-related defects.
  • Supported evaluation of dual-side tab pressing as a corrective action.
  • Coordinated feasibility checks and production setup with Equipment and the vendor.
  • Prepared the trial as a controlled process change.

Qualification and evidence

  • Supported 4M testing across multiple production lots using a defined sampling plan.
  • Collected representative samples and submitted them with supporting data to Quality.
  • Quality and Assembly confirmed an improvement in downstream defect behavior, and broader implementation was planned.
  • Reviewed downstream defects, Quality's assessment, and production behavior to support the implementation decision.

07

Physical improvements: design and installation

Two anode-line problems required physical improvements because setting changes introduced other issues. Work covered CAD modeling and installation of both parts. The sketches explain their function; CAD views omit proprietary dimensions, mounting details, and line identifiers.

Airflow duct cover

The problem

Humidity control relied on duct airflow. On newer lines, differences in the layout meant that increasing airflow directed too much air across the tab zone, causing the tabs to flutter.

The fix

Designed and installed a duct cover to redirect air away from the tab zone while preserving humidity-control airflow through a lower opening. Tab flutter decreased noticeably after installation.

The improvement was observed on the line; a measured before-and-after rate is not available for publication.

Mis-threading guard bar

The problem

During semi-automatic splicing at the unloader bobbin, the web was sometimes threaded upward instead of downward. This caused breakage and quality issues in subsequent steps.

The fix

Designed and installed a guard bar that blocked the upper route, leaving only the correct threading path open. This error-proofing approach, or poka-yoke, reduced reliance on operator familiarity under time pressure.

Before-and-after counts are not published. The sketch illustrates how the guard prevents incorrect threading; the CAD view shows its shape.

08

Incomplete cuts in the acceleration zone

Incomplete cuts appeared in a cathode line's acceleration zone. Process adjustments did not resolve the defect, but a physical check and equipment-log review narrowed the investigation. A vendor specialist diagnosed and repaired the focusing equipment the next day.

Problem

Incomplete cuts repeatedly appeared in the acceleration zone as line speed increased. Line observation confirmed where the defect developed.

Suction became less likely

After suction pressure and position adjustments, a manual separation check showed that the material had not been cut cleanly. This made suction less likely as the primary cause and directed the investigation toward the laser.

Process adjustments had no effect

Power and frequency adjustments did not move the defect, and neither did a focus adjustment.

What the equipment log showed

The laser PC log showed a missing power response in the acceleration zone. It did not identify the failed component, but the lack of response to process adjustments redirected the investigation toward the optical and focusing hardware.

How it was handled

The investigation continued through the day with Process Technology engineers and vendor staff. Process adjustments had no meaningful effect. The following day, a vendor laser specialist diagnosed and repaired the focusing equipment.

Investigation takeaways

A quick physical check can narrow the search

A brief material-separation check narrowed the likely causes before further process changes. Simple diagnostic checks can reduce unnecessary trials.

When adjustments have no effect, review equipment data

Power, frequency, and focus adjustments had no effect. The missing power response in the laser log provided a more useful lead, showing why equipment data should be reviewed earlier when recipe trials produce no response.

Document the evidence for a specialist handoff

The evidence pointed toward the focusing hardware, and repair required a vendor specialist. A useful handoff includes completed tests, their results, and relevant log evidence.

Set a clear point for escalation

Further trials added little once adjustments stopped providing new information. A defined escalation point would bring specialist support into a similar investigation earlier.

Follow-up

The key opportunity was earlier review of the laser log. Checking equipment data and escalating once process trials stopped adding information could have shortened the investigation.

09

Methods carried forward

This work reinforced three habits for investigating problems across connected process steps.

Think across process steps

A visible defect can be the late result of a process, mechanical, material or equipment condition that started earlier in the flow.

Build and verify the window

DOE worked best when each change had a clear hypothesis, a measurable response and repeatable validation across operating conditions.

Close the loop downstream

A local improvement needed confirmation through Quality review and downstream production results.

The duct cover illustrates a practical trade-off: when a setting change creates another quality issue, a physical improvement may address both needs.

Relevant methods for semiconductor manufacturing include tracing variation across steps, combining equipment and inspection evidence, and qualifying changes through downstream results.
